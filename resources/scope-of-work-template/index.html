What Belongs in a Commercial Cleaning Scope of Work

The document that separates a cleaning contract from a cleaning guess.

Why the scope document matters

A commercial cleaning contract without a scope of work is a price for an undefined service. "Nightly cleaning of 5,000 square feet" tells you what you're paying and how much space will be cleaned, but nothing about what gets cleaned, how often, at what standard, or what happens when it isn't done. The scope document fills that gap. It's the document that lets you hold a cleaning company accountable, and it's the document that protects the cleaning company from unrealistic expectations.

What a complete scope document contains

A well-written scope of work for a commercial cleaning contract covers the following sections:

Room inventory

A list of every room or area to be cleaned, with dimensions or a floor plan reference. Private offices, open-plan areas, conference rooms, restrooms, kitchens, breakrooms, reception areas, server rooms, storage rooms, and common corridors should all appear. Areas explicitly excluded from cleaning should also be listed.

Task list by area

For each area, the specific tasks to be performed on each visit. Not "clean the restrooms" — "disinfect toilet bowl, seat, and exterior; disinfect sink, faucet, and countertop; clean mirror; empty waste receptacle and replace liner; replenish soap and paper products; disinfect door handle and light switch; damp-mop floor." The specificity is what makes the document enforceable.

Frequency per task

Some tasks happen every visit. Others happen weekly, monthly, or quarterly. Grout scrubbing, high dusting, refrigerator cleaning, and blind cleaning are common tasks that should be scheduled but that often fall through the cracks when frequency isn't specified. The scope document should note the frequency alongside each task.

Floor care schedule

Floor care tasks — burnishing, scrub and recoat, strip and wax, carpet extraction — are distinct from nightly cleaning and should have their own schedule. In Minnesota, the strip-and-wax cycle should account for the road-salt season. A program that strips in October and doesn't refinish until April is leaving floors unprotected through the highest-damage months.

Product specifications

Medical facilities, food-adjacent spaces, and facilities with specific surface requirements should list which cleaning products are approved and which are prohibited. Incompatible products can damage surfaces or create compliance issues.

Access and security protocols

Key or code access, alarm arming and disarming procedures, areas that require escort, and any security log-in requirements should all be documented. This section protects both parties in the event of a security incident.

Quality review procedure

How problems get reported, how they get resolved, and what the timeline for resolution is. A scope document without a quality review procedure is incomplete.
